How British culture changed

The short version

British culture changed four times over about 130 years. Each time, more people were let in.

1890s The music hall. Culture happened in a crowd. You could join in, but you could not make it.
1930s The radio. The whole country heard the same thing on the same night. A few people in London chose it.
1960s The record player. Music went upstairs. Teenagers got a culture adults could not hear.
Now The phone. Anyone can make it, and nobody has to give permission.

Each change made culture cheaper. The habits underneath it, like the tea and the queue and the football, barely moved at all.

Below, the same thing at four lengths. Read the first one. Say the second one out loud. Use the others if you want them.

01 · the main read

Four changes

The whole story, told once. About 600 words.

1895: a crowd

In 1895 a boy pays a few pennies to get into a music hall in London.

The room is packed. The singer sings the chorus once.

Then the whole room sings it back.

That was culture. Loud, cheap, and standing up with hundreds of strangers.

Nothing he did on a Saturday happened on his own. There was the football, at a club his local factory had started. There were stories that cost a penny. Once a year there was the train to Blackpool with everyone else from his street.

None of it cost much. All of it happened in public.

But he could not make any of it. To be heard you needed a stage, and somebody else owned the stage.

1935: one radio

Then it came indoors.

The BBC started broadcasting in 1922. Within ten years most front rooms had a radio.

One radio. One family. One programme. Nobody chose it and nobody could pause it, and if you missed it, you had missed it.

Before the radio, no stranger's voice had ever come into an ordinary house.

The cinema did the same job. People went twice a week, and sat through a newsreel, a cartoon and two films for one price. In 1935 you could buy a paperback for the price of a packet of cigarettes.

So the audience got huge, and it got national. A girl in Aberdeen and a boy in Cornwall laughed at the same joke on the same night.

But a few people in London picked the joke.

1965: a door you could shut

The record player changed the furniture, not the music.

A single cost six shillings and it played upstairs, with the door shut. For the first time, teenagers had something adults could not hear. They had their own money to spend on it, too.

What they bought was already less English than before. The Beatles came out of Liverpool. Caribbean families brought ska and reggae to British cities.

Pop was wanted so badly that stations played it illegally from ships off the coast, until the BBC gave in and started Radio 1.

The gate was still there. A record label still had to say yes.

But now the labels followed young people instead of telling them.

After that: it stops being one thing

Punk needed three chords and nobody's permission. Notting Hill Carnival grew from a street party into two million people. Curry houses became the restaurants the country actually ate in.

Then in 2003 a teenager from east London made a record on cheap software in his bedroom and won the Mercury Prize with it. Grime needed no studio, no label and no stage.

Now: the phone

Now it is all in your pocket.

It is the first one that does both jobs. It plays everything ever recorded, and it records the next thing. You can make something this afternoon and have strangers watching it tonight, and nobody has to say yes.

So who chose the songs in 1895? The man who owned the hall.

In 1935? The BBC.

In 1965? A record label.

And now?

Not nobody. Something decides what gets seen, and none of us can see it. Being allowed to publish is not the same as being heard.

The shared night has gone as well. The country used to watch one thing at one time, because there was only one thing.

But the direction is clear.

In 1895 you sang along to a song somebody else picked, in a hall somebody else owned. Now you pick it, and you can write the next one.

British culture did not get better or worse. It got cheaper to join in.

02 · read aloud

Two minutes, out loud

The same four changes, written to be spoken. About 250 words.

I want to tell you how British culture changed. I am going to do it with four things.

The first one is the music hall.

In the 1890s, culture happened in a crowd. You sang along with three hundred strangers. You went to the football at a club your local factory had started. You read stories that cost a penny.

All of it was cheap. None of it was yours. You could join in, but you could not make it.

The second is the radio.

From the 1920s there was one in the corner of the room. One radio, one family, one programme. You could not choose it and you could not pause it. So the whole country heard the same thing on the same night. But a few people in London picked what it was.

The third is the record player.

In the 1960s, music could go upstairs. A single cost six shillings and it played in your bedroom with the door shut. That is the real change of the sixties. Teenagers got something adults could not hear.

But you still needed a record label to say yes.

The fourth is the phone in my pocket.

It is the first one that does both jobs. It plays every song ever recorded. And it records the next one. I could make something tonight and have strangers watching it by morning, and nobody has to give me permission.

So that is the pattern. Every time one of them arrived, culture got cheaper and more people were let in.

03 · four voices

Being 14, four times

The same age in four different years. Good for reading in pairs.

1895

I left school last year, so I work now. On a Saturday there is the football, or the music hall if I have the pennies. The singer does the chorus once and then we all do it. Nothing I do happens on my own, and nothing at all happens in my bedroom.

1935

The pictures, twice a week. At home there is the radio, and we sit round it together because there is only one and Dad picks. Tomorrow everyone at school will have heard the same programme, so we will all talk about the same thing.

1965

I have my own money and my own records, and that is the difference. A single costs six shillings and I play it upstairs with the door shut, which my parents mind more than the music. There is something now that belongs to us, and the adults do not get it.

Now

Everything ever recorded is on my phone. I watch things nobody in my class has heard of. I can make a track tonight and put it up for free, and nobody has to let me. Whether anyone sees it is decided by something none of us can see.

04 · the other half

What did not change

Works as an ending to any of the three above. About 200 words.

It would be tidy to say everything changed. It did not.

People still drink tea, and still use it to say things they would rather not say out loud. A cup is how you apologise. It is how you say well done, and how you say I have noticed you are upset.

Strangers still open with the weather. Not because it is interesting. Because it is safe.

People still queue, and a queue-jumper still gets a tut and a cold look instead of a word.

There is still a roast on a Sunday, and fireworks on the fifth of November.

Football is the clearest case. The clubs that formed in the 1880s are the same clubs. Which one you support is still decided by where you were born, and it still cannot be changed. Everything around it moved: the money, the television, the players, the price of a ticket. The thing in the middle did not move at all.

So that is the honest version. Those four changes changed who was allowed to make British culture, and it turned out a lot more people wanted to. The tea and the queue stayed exactly where they were.
