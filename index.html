Britain · group poster · four people

Your quarter is A4. Your text is 900 words.

Two things get handed in. A quarter of the group poster, which is what people look at, and at least 900 words of your own writing, which is what gets marked. This page has the plan for both.

The whole poster, and your bit of it

Two A3 sheets side by side make one poster, 840 mm wide. Split into four, each person gets A4: 210 × 297 mm.

Four panels in a row

Yours sits next to three others. Whatever you do, the person beside you is doing too, so agree the rules before anybody starts.

Agree these five things

Border width, title height, which pen, which colours, and where the year column goes. Write them down.

About 90 words on the panel

A4 holds roughly double an A5. That is a title, five or six rows, and one drawing. Not your 900 words.

The 900 words are separate

They are handed in as text. The panel is the short version of the same argument.

Marks are for discussion

"I will be looking at the level of detail and discussion." Listing facts scores low. Explaining why they matter scores high.

Bullet points, then sentences

You may plan in bullets. What you hand in must be full sentences, spellchecked and proofread.

00

How to hit 900 words without padding

The problem: 900 words is a lot if you list facts, and not much at all if you discuss them. Four facts, properly discussed, will fill it.

  1. Pick four points about your theme. Four is enough. Six will make every one of them shallow, and shallow is what loses marks.
  2. Give each point 200 words, in three parts: say it, prove it with a fact or a date, then say why it matters.
  3. That third part is the discussion the teacher is marking. It is usually two or three sentences beginning "This mattered because…" or "The result was…".
  4. Write the introduction last, once you know what your four points turned out to be.
  5. Read the whole thing out loud before you hand it in. Every sentence you stumble over is one to rewrite.
Listing versus discussing — the same fact, twice
LISTING (low marks, 18 words) Punk started in Britain in 1976. It was loud and fast and only used three chords. DISCUSSING (better, 74 words) Punk arrived in Britain in 1976, and what made it matter was not the noise but the three chords. Learning three chords takes an afternoon, so for the first time you did not need lessons, money or a record company to start a band. Hundreds of teenagers did. This is why punk changed British music far more than it changed British listening: it changed who was allowed to make it.
The rule

If a sentence only tells the reader something happened, add a sentence saying what it changed. That is how 40 words becomes 200 honestly.

Words that start discussion

"This mattered because…" · "The result was…" · "Before this, you had to…" · "It is easy to miss that…" · "Compared with…"

Do not pad

No "in today's modern society". A teacher spots it instantly and it costs you more than the words gain.

01

Music

The one big point your 900 words should make: every time British music changed, it got cheaper to make, so more people were allowed to make it.

Your four points
POINT 1 · 200 WORDS

You had to be in the room.

Prove it with: Music hall in the 1890s. Marie Lloyd, the biggest star in England, singing to a packed room. There were no recordings, so a song only existed while somebody was singing it.

Then discuss: Nobody owned a copy of anything. Music was something you went to, with hundreds of strangers, or it did not happen at all. Compare that with hearing a song alone on headphones.

POINT 2 · 200 WORDS

The record player made music private.

Prove it with: The Beatles, Liverpool, 1962. A single cost about six shillings and played in your own bedroom.

Then discuss: This is the first time teenagers had a culture their parents could not hear. Ask why that mattered: what changes when the people making the music are aiming at you and not at your family?

POINT 3 · 200 WORDS

Punk proved you did not need permission.

Prove it with: 1976. Three chords, which takes an afternoon to learn.

Then discuss: Before punk you needed lessons, an instrument and a record company. Afterwards, hundreds of teenagers started bands in a year. It changed who was allowed to make music more than it changed what music sounded like.

POINT 4 · 200 WORDS

Now the studio is a bedroom.

Prove it with: Grime came out of Bow in east London around 2002, made on cheap computers. Stormzy headlined Glastonbury in 2019.

Then discuss: Nobody has to say yes any more. But something replaced the record company: an algorithm decides what gets heard, and nobody can see how. Is that more free, or just differently controlled?

On the A4 panel
MADE IN BRITAIN 1890s MUSIC HALL You had to be in the room. 1962 THE BEATLES Liverpool. 1976 PUNK Three chords, no permission. 2002 GRIME Made in a bedroom in Bow. 2019 STORMZY Headlined Glastonbury. 1890: you needed a stage somebody owned. 2002: you needed a secondhand computer. About 45 words. A4 will take one or two more rows than A5 would, but not a paragraph.
Best fact

The Beatles started as a skiffle band, playing a washboard and a box with a string on it. Cheap instruments, again.

Worth looking up

Glastonbury 1970: £1 a ticket with free milk, 1,500 people. Now about £400 and 210,000 people, on the same Somerset dairy farm.

Do not overlap

Leave the films to whoever has that theme, even though the Beatles made films too.

02

Traditions and holidays

The one big point your 900 words should make: about half of what feels like ancient British tradition was invented recently, and people cannot tell which half.

Your four points
POINT 1 · 200 WORDS

A tradition can outlive its reason.

Prove it with: Bonfire Night, 5 November 1605. A group tried to blow up Parliament and failed.

Then discuss: Britain has marked a failed crime for 420 years. Most people setting off fireworks could not tell you what happened. Ask what a tradition is once nobody remembers why they are doing it.

POINT 2 · 200 WORDS

Some traditions were invented to sell something.

Prove it with: The Christmas card was invented in London in 1843 by Henry Cole, who had them printed and sold.

Then discuss: It feels old and warm and it started as a product. This is worth discussing carefully: it does not make the tradition fake, but it does mean traditions are made by people, not handed down from nowhere.

POINT 3 · 200 WORDS

Immigration adds traditions, and they become British.

Prove it with: Notting Hill Carnival began in 1966, started by London's Caribbean community. It now draws around two million people and is the biggest street festival in Europe.

Then discuss: In under sixty years it went from a small local event to something the whole country recognises. What makes a tradition British — how old it is, or how many people keep it?

POINT 4 · 200 WORDS

New ones are still arriving.

Prove it with: Trick or treating arrived in Britain from America in the 1980s. Your grandparents did not do it.

Then discuss: It already feels permanent. If a tradition can become normal in forty years, then the ones that feel ancient were once new too, and somebody at the time probably complained about them.

On the A4 panel
THE BRITISH YEAR 1605 BONFIRE NIGHT Someone tried to blow up Parliament. 1843 CHRISTMAS CARDS Invented in London. 1966 NOTTING HILL Two million people. CARNIVAL 1980s TRICK OR TREATING Arrived from America. Half of it is older than you think. Half of it is newer.
Best fact

Everyone lets off fireworks on 5 November and almost nobody can tell you why.

Worth looking up

Burns Night in Scotland, first held in 1801. Pancake Day, which is genuinely medieval. Diwali in Leicester, one of the biggest outside India.

Strong ending

Ask the reader which of your four they thought was oldest. Most people guess Christmas cards.

03

Literature

The one big point your 900 words should make: each big moment in British writing made reading cheaper or easier, until anyone could do it — and then anyone could write too.

Your four points
POINT 1 · 200 WORDS

Chaucer wrote in English when serious books were not.

Prove it with: The Canterbury Tales, around 1387. Latin and French were the languages of serious writing.

Then discuss: He was choosing the language ordinary people actually spoke. That decision is part of why English became a language worth writing books in at all. It is a bigger deal than it sounds.

POINT 2 · 200 WORDS

Shakespeare built the language while using it.

Prove it with: Around 1,700 words are first written down in his plays: lonely, eyeball, bedroom, swagger. His theatre had standing tickets for a penny.

Then discuss: Two things at once: he was writing for everybody, not just the educated, and he was inventing the words as he went. Discuss what it means that the most famous English writer was writing popular entertainment.

POINT 3 · 200 WORDS

Dickens made books affordable by selling them in pieces.

Prove it with: From 1836 he published novels a chapter at a time, monthly, cheaply.

Then discuss: People who could never afford a whole book could afford one chapter. This changed who novels were written for, and you can see it in the stories: cliffhangers, because he needed you to buy the next part.

POINT 4 · 200 WORDS

Cheap printing, then no gatekeeper at all.

Prove it with: Penguin paperbacks, 1935, sixpence — the price of a packet of cigarettes. Harry Potter, 1997, turned down by twelve publishers first.

Then discuss: The twelve rejections are the interesting bit. Even in 1997 somebody still had to say yes. Now anyone can publish online without permission, which raises the same question as music: who decides what gets read?

On the A4 panel
ONE SHELF, 600 YEARS 1 1387 CHAUCER Written in English, not Latin. 2 1600 SHAKESPEARE 1,700 brand new words. 3 1836 DICKENS Sold a chapter at a time. 4 1935 PENGUIN A book for sixpence. 5 1997 HARRY POTTER Turned down twelve times. Every one made books cheaper or easier to get.
Best fact

Nobody had written down the words lonely, eyeball or bedroom before Shakespeare did.

Worth looking up

The Brontë sisters published under men's names in 1847. Sherlock Holmes, 1887. The Mousetrap has run in London since 1952.

Do not overlap

The Harry Potter films belong to the films theme. Stick to the books.

04

Actors and films

The one big point your 900 words should make: Britain has spent a century training film talent that ends up working for America, and British actors now play most of the American heroes.

Your four points
POINT 1 · 200 WORDS

Britain made the director, then lost him.

Prove it with: Alfred Hitchcock, born in Leytonstone in east London, made his first thriller in 1927 and worked in Britain for over ten years before moving to Hollywood in 1939.

Then discuss: This is the pattern for the whole century. Britain trains people and America can pay them more. Is that a failure of the British film industry, or a sign of how good its training is?

POINT 2 · 200 WORDS

Bond sells an idea of Britain to the world.

Prove it with: Dr No, 1962. Still running more than sixty years later, and one of the most successful film series ever made.

Then discuss: Bond is not what Britain is like. He is what Britain would like to be seen as: clever, well dressed, and still important. Discuss the difference between a country and its export version.

POINT 3 · 200 WORDS

The films are British-made even when they do not feel it.

Prove it with: All eight Harry Potter films were made in Britain, at Leavesden Studios near Watford, employing thousands of people.

Then discuss: This is film as an industry, not just as art: sets, crews, jobs, money. Worth arguing that the studios matter to Britain more than any single film does.

POINT 4 · 200 WORDS

Now Britain supplies the heroes.

Prove it with: Spider-Man, Batman and Superman have all been played by British actors: Tom Holland, Christian Bale and Robert Pattinson, and Henry Cavill.

Then discuss: Britain has cheap drama schools and a huge theatre tradition, so it produces trained actors constantly. But they are playing America's characters, in America's films. Say what you think that tells you.

On the A4 panel
THEY'RE ALL BRITISH 1927 HITCHCOCK Made thrillers in London before Hollywood. 1962 JAMES BOND Still going 60 years later. 2001 HARRY POTTER All eight films made here. NOW THE SUPERHEROES Spider-Man, Batman and Superman have all been played by British actors. They just put the accent on.
Best fact

The superhero line. Most of your class will not know it, and it is the one people will repeat.

Check it yourself

Tom Holland, Christian Bale, Robert Pattinson, Henry Cavill. Look each one up before you write it down.

Worth looking up

Monty Python, Wallace and Gromit, and the fact that the world's first moving picture was filmed in Leeds in 1888.
