The development of culture in Britain

Who gets to make culture — and who gets to hear it

Across two hundred years the same thing keeps happening in Britain: a new machine makes culture cheaper, a wider group of people gets hold of it, and what counts as British culture changes. Four eras, four shifts in who was holding the microphone.

1800 – 1900

Culture becomes mass-produced

Steam presses, railways and — after the 1870 Education Act — a population that could read. For the first time culture is made in bulk, sold cheaply, and aimed at ordinary working people rather than a wealthy few.

People

  • Charles Dickens1812–1870 · born PortsmouthPublished novels in monthly parts so anyone could afford them, then toured Britain reading them aloud to packed halls.
  • Marie Lloyd1870–1922 · born Hoxton, LondonThe biggest star of the music hall. Sang "The Boy I Love Is Up in the Gallery" straight at the cheap seats.
  • Charlotte Brontë1816–1855 · Haworth, YorkshirePublished Jane Eyre in 1847 as "Currer Bell" — a man's name, to be taken seriously.
  • W. G. Grace1848–1915 · Downend, BristolTurned cricket into a spectator sport that drew paying crowds in the tens of thousands.
  • Gilbert & Sullivanpartnership from 1871 · LondonComic operas built for a middle-class night out; the Savoy Theatre was built for them in 1881.
  • William Morris1834–1896 · WalthamstowArts and Crafts: argued that ordinary objects and ordinary houses were culture too.

Places

  • Blackpool, LancashireTower opened 1894The mill towns' seaside. Railways and the 1871 Bank Holidays Act filled it every summer.
  • Freemasons' Tavern, LondonGreat Queen Street · 26 October 1863Where the Football Association was founded and the rules of the game were finally written down.
  • The Crystal Palace, Hyde ParkGreat Exhibition, 1851Six million visits in five months — mass culture as a national spectacle.
  • Haworth Parsonage, West Yorkshirehome of the BrontësA moorland village that produced three of the century's most-read novelists.
  • Queen's Hall, Langham Placefirst Proms, 1895Henry Wood's promenade concerts, priced so a clerk could come in after work.

Reading

Dickens publishes in monthly instalments from 1836 — a shilling at a time, so the whole country reads the same story together. Penny dreadfuls, circulating libraries and cheap newspapers follow.

Music & stage

Music hall from the 1850s. Brass bands in the mill and mining towns — Black Dyke Mills band forms in 1855 and still plays. The Proms begin in 1895, deliberately cheap.

Everyday life

The railway invents the day trip and the seaside holiday. Football's rules are written in 1863, the FA Cup follows in 1872, the Football League in 1888. Christmas is remade — cards and A Christmas Carol both arrive in 1843.

Who got heard

Working-class audiences, at scale, for the first time — and women novelists, though the Brontës and George Eliot both published under men's names.

The shift: culture stops being something you attend if you can afford it, and becomes something you buy — a serial, a match ticket, a train fare to Blackpool.

1900 – 1945

Culture gets broadcast

Cinema, recording and radio arrive within a generation, and two world wars push everybody into the same national conversation. Culture is no longer local — millions now hear the same thing at the same moment.

People

  • John Reith1889–1971 · born Stonehaven, ScotlandThe BBC's first Director-General. Set its mission: to inform, educate and entertain — in that order.
  • Alfred Hitchcock1899–1980 · born Leytonstone, LondonLearned his craft in British studios; The 39 Steps (1935) made him famous before Hollywood took him.
  • Gracie Fields1898–1979 · born above a chip shop, Rochdale"Our Gracie" — a mill-town girl who became the highest-paid film star in the world in 1937.
  • George Formby1904–1961 · WiganUkulele, grin and Lancashire accent; the most popular British entertainer of the war years.
  • Virginia Woolf1882–1941 · Bloomsbury, LondonMrs Dalloway (1925) took the novel inside people's heads and left the plot outside.
  • Allen Lane1902–1970 · founded Penguin, 1935Sixpenny paperbacks — good books for the price of a packet of cigarettes.
  • Emmeline Pankhurst1858–1928 · ManchesterFounded the WSPU in 1903; women get the vote in 1918 and on equal terms in 1928.

Places

  • Broadcasting House, Portland Placeopened 1932 · LondonBuilt as a ship for the new medium; still the BBC's front door.
  • Alexandra Palace, north London2 November 1936The world's first regular high-definition television service went out from its mast.
  • Ealing Studios, west Londonfilming since 1902The oldest continuously working film studio on earth.
  • Blackpool Tower BallroomWurlitzer installed 1935Dance bands and a national dance craze, broadcast live on the wireless.
  • Bloomsbury, Londonthe Bloomsbury GroupA few streets that produced Woolf, Keynes and a whole argument about modern art.

Screen

Cinema becomes the national habit — by the late 1930s Britain buys roughly 20 million cinema tickets a week. Hitchcock, Gracie Fields and George Formby are the first mass-market British stars.

Radio

The BBC begins in 1922. Wartime bulletins, the Shipping Forecast and comedy like ITMA become shared national furniture.

Page & music

Penguin, 1935: serious books for sixpence. Woolf breaks the novel apart in Bloomsbury; Vaughan Williams tramps round villages writing folk songs down before they vanish.

Who got heard

Women, after 1918 and 1928. Regional accents, through Fields and Formby. Jazz arrives from America and Black musicians play London clubs.

The shift: from many local cultures to one national one. There is now such a thing as what "the country" watched, read and listened to last night.

1950 – 1979

Culture belongs to the young

Post-war wages, near-full employment and the arrival of the teenager as a paying customer. Add television in the front room and the Windrush generation from 1948, and British culture stops taking its orders from the top.

People

  • The BeatlesLiverpool · "Love Me Do", October 1962Four working-class Liverpudlians who rewrote what a pop group was allowed to be.
  • Claudia Jones1915–1964 · Trinidad-born, based in LondonFounded the Caribbean carnival in 1959 that grew into Notting Hill Carnival.
  • Mary Quant1930–2023 · shop on the King's Road, 1955Made the miniskirt, and made London a place people came to for clothes.
  • Delia Derbyshire1937–2001 · Coventry · BBC Radiophonic WorkshopBuilt the Doctor Who theme in 1963 out of tape loops and a plucked wire — electronic music before synthesisers.
  • David Bowie1947–2016 · born Brixton, raised BromleyZiggy Stardust in 1972 gave a generation permission to reinvent themselves.
  • John Osborne1929–1994 · Royal Court, 1956Look Back in Anger put an angry working-class voice on the London stage.
  • Vivienne Westwood & Malcolm McLaren430 King's Road, from 1971Their shop dressed the Sex Pistols and invented what punk looked like.

Places

  • The Cavern Club, Mathew Street, Liverpoolthe Beatles played there ~292 timesA cellar under a fruit warehouse; the loudest argument for provincial Britain ever made.
  • Abbey Road Studios, St John's WoodLondonWhere Sgt. Pepper was built — and where the studio itself became an instrument.
  • Notting Hill, west Londonon the streets from 1966Now Europe's biggest street carnival, drawing around two million people.
  • Carnaby Street & the King's Road"Swinging London", mid-1960sTwo streets that exported British youth style to the world.
  • Brighton seafrontWhitsun 1964Mods and rockers fighting on the beach — the first youth culture treated as a national emergency.
  • Granada Studios, ManchesterCoronation Street, from 1960A northern street on national television, week after week, for decades.

Music

Skiffle, then rock'n'roll, then the Beatles in 1962. Caribbean sound systems bring ska and reggae to Birmingham, Bristol and London. Pirate ships force the BBC to launch Radio 1 in 1967. Punk in 1976 tells everyone they can start a band tonight.

Television

The 1953 coronation sells the sets. ITV in 1955 brings adverts and competition; Coronation Street 1960, Doctor Who 1963, satire on That Was The Week That Was, colour from 1967.

Stage & page

Kitchen-sink realism puts ordinary northern lives on stage and screen — Look Back in Anger (1956), Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960). Regional accents stop being something to lose.

Who got heard

Teenagers with wages. Working-class writers and actors. Black British culture, moving from front rooms to the street with Notting Hill Carnival from 1966.

The shift: the audience starts making the culture. Style tribes — teds, mods, rockers, skinheads, hippies, punks — are invented from below and sold back upwards.

1980 – today

Everyone becomes a broadcaster

Cheap studios, then cheap distribution, then a phone in every pocket. The shared national moment breaks into millions of separate feeds — and the people who were once the audience now publish directly.

People

  • Dizzee Rascalborn 1984 · Bow, east LondonBoy in da Corner won the Mercury Prize in 2003 — grime's arrival, made on a school computer.
  • Stormzyborn 1993 · Thornton Heath, CroydonFirst Black British solo artist to headline Glastonbury, 2019. Started on YouTube freestyles.
  • Adeleborn 1988 · Tottenham, LondonWent to the BRIT School in Croydon; 21 (2011) became one of the best-selling albums ever made.
  • Danny Boyleborn 1956 · Radcliffe, Greater ManchesterTrainspotting (1996), then the London 2012 opening ceremony — the NHS, Windrush and Dizzee Rascal in one stadium.
  • Zadie Smithborn 1975 · Willesden, north LondonWhite Teeth (2000) wrote multicultural London as simply the way the country is.
  • Michaela Coelborn 1987 · Hackney, LondonI May Destroy You (2020) — written, co-directed and starred in by the same person.
  • Jazzie Bborn 1963 · Hornsey, LondonSoul II Soul grew out of a north London sound system into a global sound.

Places

  • Bow, east Londongrime, early 2000sTower blocks, pirate radio aerials and Rinse FM. A scene built with no industry behind it.
  • The Haçienda, Manchester1982–1997Factory Records' club; where acid house turned into a national youth movement in 1988.
  • Wembley StadiumLive Aid, 13 July 1985Watched by an estimated 1.5 billion people — the last truly shared broadcast moment.
  • Worthy Farm, Pilton, SomersetGlastonbury, since 1970A dairy farm that became the biggest greenfield festival in the world.
  • Goldsmiths, New Crossthe Young British Artists, from 1988An art school in south London that changed what British art was for.
  • Olympic Stadium, Stratford27 July 2012Britain told its own story to the world — and put the NHS and Windrush at the centre of it.

Music

Two-tone out of Coventry; Live Aid 1985; acid house and rave from 1988; Britpop's Blur-versus-Oasis summer in 1995; then grime from east London, UK drill, and Stormzy at Glastonbury in 2019.

Screen

Channel 4 arrives in 1982 with a remit to commission outsiders. Then satellite, then streaming: Bake Off, Fleabag, I May Destroy You — but almost nobody watches them at the same hour any more.

Everyday life

Chicken tikka masala is called "a true British national dish" by Foreign Secretary Robin Cook in 2001. More than 300 languages are spoken in London. Festivals become a summer institution.

Who got heard

Anyone with a laptop. Bedroom producers and YouTubers bypass the gatekeepers entirely — and Black British and South Asian voices shape the mainstream rather than sitting beside it.

The shift: the gatekeepers lose control of distribution. The question stops being "will they let me on?" and becomes "can I find an audience?"
