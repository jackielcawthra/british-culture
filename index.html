Britain · what people actually do

British culture is mostly small things, done by everyone

Culture isn't only museums and kings. It's what you eat, what you watch, what you shout at a match, and the words you use with your mates. Here's what British culture looks like now — and how it got that way.

What British culture actually looks like

Nine everyday things

Ask someone in Britain to describe their culture and they'll probably shrug. It's easier to spot from the outside. These are the things almost everyone there does, eats or argues about.

Tea, constantly

Britain drinks around 100 million cups a day. Tea is also a way of saying things — offering someone a cup is how you say sorry, well done, or that you noticed they're upset.

Football, seriously

The rules were written down in a London pub in 1863 and the game went everywhere. Which club you support is usually decided by where you were born, and you can't change it.

Chips with everything

Fish and chips, chips and gravy, chips and curry sauce. Britain's most popular takeaway is now curry — a Foreign Secretary once called chicken tikka masala a true British national dish.

Talking about weather

Not because it's dramatic — because it's safe. It's how strangers start a conversation without having to be personal. Complaining about it together counts as bonding.

Queueing, properly

An orderly line is close to a rule. Push in and nobody will shout — you'll get a tut, a sigh and a very cold look, which is worse.

Carnival and festivals

Notting Hill Carnival fills west London every August with Caribbean sound systems and about two million people. Glastonbury takes over a Somerset dairy farm every summer.

The Sunday roast

Meat, roast potatoes, too many vegetables and gravy, eaten late on a Sunday with family. Yorkshire puddings are non-negotiable, even when the meat isn't beef.

Bonfire Night

Every 5 November, fireworks and a bonfire — marking a failed attempt to blow up Parliament in 1605. Most people setting off the fireworks couldn't tell you that.

Taking the mickey

British humour is dry, sarcastic and usually aimed at yourself. Insulting your friends affectionately is called banter, and being too serious about yourself is the real crime.

It isn't one country

Four nations

Great Britain is England, Scotland and Wales. The United Kingdom adds Northern Ireland. They share a language and a football rivalry, and not much else.

England

Morris dancing, cricket on a village green, the Proms, pantomime at Christmas, and a lot of arguing about the correct way to make tea.

Scotland

Burns Night every January with haggis and poetry read out loud. Ceilidh dancing, bagpipes, Highland games, and Gaelic still spoken in the islands.

Wales

Welsh is a living language taught in schools. The Eisteddfod is a national festival of music and poetry, and male voice choirs are a real thing, not a stereotype.

Northern Ireland

Traditional music sessions in pubs, the Ulster fry for breakfast, Van Morrison, and Derry Girls — which did more for its image than any advert.

1890s

Being 14 in the 1890s

You've probably already left school and started work. Fun costs pennies and happens in a crowd — nothing is private, and nothing happens in your bedroom.

Music

The music hall — singing, jokes and beer in the same room. A piano in the front room at home. A brass band in the park on Sunday, made up of men from the local mill or mine.

Stories

Penny dreadfuls: cheap, gory adventure stories sold weekly to boys, and disapproved of by every adult. Dickens novels came out a chapter at a time, like a series drop.

Saturday

The football. Clubs were being started by factories, churches and pubs, and by 1888 there was a proper league. Or the train to Blackpool for the day if you could afford it.

Looking the part

A flat cap and a neckerchief if you worked. Your good clothes came out on Sunday. Nobody dressed differently because they were young — you dressed like a smaller adult.

Names worth knowing Marie Lloyd — the biggest music hall star, who sang cheeky songs straight at the cheap seats. Charles Dickens — sold novels in monthly parts so ordinary people could afford them. W. G. Grace — the huge bearded cricketer who made sport something you paid to watch. Places: Blackpool, the Canterbury music hall in Lambeth, Haworth in Yorkshire where the Brontë sisters wrote.

1930s

Being 14 in the 1930s

There's a radio in the corner of the room and a cinema at the end of the street. For the first time the whole country sees and hears the same thing on the same night.

Watching

The cinema, twice a week. By the late 1930s Britain bought around 20 million tickets a week. You'd sit through the newsreel, a cartoon and two films for the same money.

Listening

The BBC started in 1922. Comedy shows, news and dance bands, all live. Nobody had ever heard a stranger's voice inside their own house before.

Reading

Penguin paperbacks arrived in 1935 costing sixpence — the same as a packet of cigarettes. Suddenly a proper book wasn't a posh object.

Saturday

Football in the afternoon, the dance hall in the evening. Big band music, and dances everyone learned the steps to. Blackpool Tower Ballroom was the most famous floor in the country.

Names worth knowing Gracie Fields — born above a chip shop in Rochdale, became the best-paid film star in the world. George Formby — Wigan, a ukulele and a grin; the country's favourite entertainer. Alfred Hitchcock — from Leytonstone in east London, learned to make thrillers here before Hollywood. Allen Lane — invented the cheap paperback. Places: Broadcasting House in London, Alexandra Palace, where the world's first proper TV broadcast went out in 1936.

1960s

Being 14 in the 1960s

You have your own money, your own records and — for the first time in history — your own culture that adults don't understand and aren't invited to.

Music

The Beatles from Liverpool in 1962, then everyone else. Caribbean families brought ska and reggae to British cities. Pirate radio ships broadcast pop illegally until the BBC gave in and started Radio 1 in 1967.

Telly

Doctor Who from 1963 — watched from behind the sofa. Top of the Pops every week. Coronation Street, set on a normal northern street, which had never been on TV before.

Clothes

This is when youth style is invented. Mods on scooters in sharp suits, rockers on motorbikes in leather. Mary Quant's miniskirts on the King's Road. What you wore said which side you were on.

Saturday

Town, with your mates and no adults. Record shop, coffee bar, dance. Then in 1966 the first Caribbean carnival on the streets of Notting Hill.

Names worth knowing The Beatles — four working-class Liverpudlians who changed what a band could be. Claudia Jones — started the Caribbean carnival that grew into Notting Hill Carnival. Delia Derbyshire — made the Doctor Who theme out of tape loops, before synthesisers existed. Mary Quant — the miniskirt, and London as a place people came to for clothes. Places: the Cavern Club in Liverpool, Abbey Road, Carnaby Street.

Now

Being 14 today

The phone in your pocket is a record shop, a cinema, a radio station and a recording studio. You don't just pick the culture — you can make it and put it out yourself.

Music

Grime came out of Bow in east London in the early 2000s, made on school computers. Then UK drill, Afroswing and whatever's next. British rap sounds British — the accents are the point.

Watching

Everything streams, so almost nobody watches at the same time. The exceptions still matter: a big match, a final, Bake Off — the last few things the whole country does at once.

Food

Curry, kebabs, jollof, bubble tea, a chippy tea and a Sunday roast — all normal, all British. Britain's food is basically a record of who moved there.

Saturday

Football, town, a festival in summer, Carnival in August. And a group chat running through all of it, which is where most of the actual culture happens.

Names worth knowing Stormzy — from Croydon, started with YouTube freestyles, first Black British solo artist to headline Glastonbury in 2019. Dizzee Rascal — made Boy in da Corner as a teenager in Bow and won the Mercury Prize with it. Adele — Tottenham, went to the BRIT School. Michaela Coel — from Hackney, wrote and starred in her own series. Places: Bow, Notting Hill, Glastonbury, Wembley.

How the slang changed

Same job, different words

Every generation invents words their parents don't use, mostly so their parents don't understand them. British slang also changes by city — Liverpool, Glasgow and London don't sound alike at all.

1890s

  • chuffed — really pleased
  • toff — a rich, posh person
  • bobby — a police officer
  • bloke — a man (still going strong)

1930s

  • wizard — brilliant, excellent
  • blighty — Britain, said fondly
  • swell — great
  • cheerio — goodbye

1960s

  • fab — fantastic
  • gear — very good
  • grotty — grim, horrible
  • naff — rubbish, uncool

Now

  • peng — attractive, excellent
  • bare — loads of
  • long — too much effort
  • safe — thanks, or all good
