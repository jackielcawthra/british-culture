Britain · poster ideas

Four posters, four British things

Music, dry humour, the pub, and books. Each poster takes one of them and shows how it grew into what it is now. Layout, exact words, and what to draw. Pick one.

Before you start

A poster is read from three metres away by someone walking past. That is the whole brief. Everything below comes from it.

One idea only

A poster with six ideas on it says nothing. Pick one thing and put it in the middle.

Six words in the title

Write it as big as your hand. If it does not fit, the title is too long.

Dates down one edge

On a poster about how something developed, line the years up in a single column. It does half the work.

No paragraphs

Nothing longer than one line. Nobody standing up reads a paragraph.

Numbers beat adjectives

"210,000 people" is better than "a huge festival".

Draw it yourself

A wonky drawing you made beats a printed photo. Teachers can tell instantly.

01

Music

How it developed: every twenty years or so a new British sound appears, and almost every time it comes from a poorer town and cheaper equipment than the one before.

  1. Title: MADE IN BRITAIN. Draw a record beside it — two circles and a dot.
  2. Rule one line straight down the page, about a fifth of the way across. This is the timeline and everything hangs off it.
  3. Put eight dots on the line, evenly spaced. Measure them, do not guess.
  4. Years to the left of the line, in one neat column. Name and one line to the right.
  5. The two lines at the bottom are the point of the whole poster. Leave room for them.
The exact words
MADE IN BRITAIN Every sound came from somewhere cheaper 1890s MUSIC HALL A packed room, all singing along. 1930s DANCE BANDS On the radio. The BBC chose them. 1956 SKIFFLE A washboard and a tea chest. Nearly free. 1962 THE BEATLES Liverpool. They started as a skiffle band. 1970 HEAVY METAL Black Sabbath, Birmingham. A factory town making a factory noise. 1976 PUNK Three chords and nobody's permission. 1992 JUNGLE Made on a computer, not with a band. 2002 GRIME Bow, east London. Made in a bedroom. 1890: you needed a stage somebody else owned. 2002: you needed a secondhand computer. Glastonbury, 1970: £1 a ticket, free milk, 1,500 people. Now £400 and 210,000 people, on the same Somerset dairy farm.
What to draw

One record at the top and one straight line down the page. Eight dots. That is the entire drawing.

Why it works

The places are the surprise. Not London studios: Birmingham, Bow, Croydon, Liverpool. And the gear gets cheaper every step, which is the argument.

Best fact on it

The Beatles began as a skiffle band, playing a washboard and a box with a string on it. Put that one in bigger writing.

02

Dry humour

How it developed: the joke slowly moved. First it was about other people, then about the situation, and now it is about yourself. That is what "dry" means.

  1. Title: WHO IS THE JOKE ABOUT? That question is the whole poster.
  2. Rule three columns: when, what, and who the joke is about.
  3. Fill in seven rows, in order. The right-hand column is the one that matters, so write it in capitals.
  4. Keep the bottom third for four translations of things British people say.
  5. Finish with: Nobody smiles. That is the entire point.
The exact words
WHO IS THE JOKE ABOUT? British comedy, 400 years, getting drier WHEN WHAT THE JOKE IS ABOUT 1600 Shakespeare's fools THE KING The fool was the only one allowed to insult him. 1841 Punch magazine POLITICIANS 1890s Music hall comics THE POSH 1940 Wartime radio THE BOMBING Making a disaster sound small became a national habit. 1969 Monty Python NOTHING, ON PURPOSE 1975 Fawlty Towers A MAN LOSING IT 2001 The Office YOURSELF Remade in more than ten countries. AND THIS IS HOW IT SOUNDS NOW "A bit breezy" The fence has blown down "Not bad" Genuinely brilliant "I'm fine" I am not fine "I might be wrong" I am not wrong Nobody smiles. That is the entire point.
What to draw

Two stick figures at the bottom with straight lines for mouths. That is the joke about the joke.

Why it works

Most humour posters are just a list of funny phrases. This one shows the phrases came from somewhere, which is what a teacher is marking.

Add your own

Ask at home for two more translations. Real ones from your family beat any you find online, and you can say where you got them.

03

The pub

How it developed: a house with a bush on a pole became the middle of the village, and is now closing at about fifty a month. A thousand years in one column.

  1. Draw the front of a pub in the top corner: a box, a triangle roof, a door, two windows, and a sign hanging off a pole. Everyone will know what it is.
  2. Title: THE LOCAL, with the small line underneath about it really being somebody's house.
  3. Run the years down the left in one column, and the story down the right.
  4. Rule a faint line between each date.
  5. Make the last row stand out — 45,000 left, down from 69,000. That is the poster's ending, and it is a sad one.
The exact words
THE LOCAL A public house. It really was somebody's house. 1300s A BUSH ON A POLE Hung outside meant the ale was ready. Most people could not read a sign. 1393 SIGNS BECOME LAW The king orders every alehouse to hang one out. That is why pubs have signs, and why the pictures are so simple. 1700s COACHING INNS Beds, stables and food on every main road. The pub becomes the middle of the village. 1915 CLOSING TIME INVENTED A wartime law to keep factory workers sober. It lasted about ninety years. 2007 SMOKING BANNED Pubs start selling food instead, and the Sunday roast takes over. NOW 45,000 LEFT There were about 69,000 in 1980. Roughly fifty close every month. Quiz night. Darts. The Sunday roast. A dog. The most common pub name is The Red Lion.
What to draw

One pub front. A rectangle, a triangle on top, a door, two windows and a hanging sign. Draw your own local if you have one.

Why it works

Nobody expects a pub poster to be a thousand years long. The bush on a pole and the sign law are the facts people repeat afterwards.

Make it local

Put your nearest pub's name and its date on the poster. If it has a sign with a lion or a swan on it, that is the 1393 law still working.

04

Literature

How it developed: one shelf, left to right, 600 years. Each book made reading cheaper or easier than the one before it, until anyone could do it.

  1. Draw one shelf line across the page, and stand ten books on it. Rectangles of slightly different heights and widths. Do not try to draw covers.
  2. Number the spines 1 to 10, left to right, oldest first.
  3. Underneath, write the key: year on the left, name in capitals, one detail.
  4. Title: ONE SHELF, 600 YEARS, and under it read it left to right.
  5. Last line at the bottom: every step made a book cheaper than the last. That is the development, and it is the reason to draw them in order.
The exact words
ONE SHELF, 600 YEARS Read it left to right 1387 CHAUCER, THE CANTERBURY TALES Written in English when serious books were written in Latin or French. 1600 SHAKESPEARE About 1,700 words are first written down in his plays. Lonely. Eyeball. Bedroom. 1719 ROBINSON CRUSOE Often called the first English novel. 1813 JANE AUSTEN, PRIDE AND PREJUDICE 1836 CHARLES DICKENS Sold a chapter at a time so people who could not afford a book could afford the story. 1847 THE BRONTË SISTERS Three sisters in one Yorkshire house, published under men's names. 1887 SHERLOCK HOLMES 1935 PENGUIN PAPERBACKS Sixpence. The price of a packet of cigarettes. 1954 LORD OF THE RINGS Written by an Oxford professor who invented the languages first. 1997 HARRY POTTER Written in Edinburgh. Turned down by twelve publishers. Every step made a book cheaper or easier to get than the last one. And the theatre never stopped: The Mousetrap has been running in London since 1952.
What to draw

Ten rectangles standing on a line. Vary the heights slightly or it looks like a bar chart.

Why it works

Everybody knows the last three and nobody knows the first three, so the poster teaches something while still being recognisable.

Best fact on it

Words like lonely, eyeball and bedroom are first written down in Shakespeare. Say a few out loud when you present it.

How big to write. On an A3 poster: title about 8 cm tall, headings about 2 cm, everything else about 1 cm. Stand back three metres. If you cannot read the smallest line, nobody else will either.

Pencil first, always. Rule every line, pencil every letter, then go over it. A tidy poster with one idea beats a clever poster that looks rushed.

Check your dates. Look up every date before you write it out, because a teacher will check one of them. Great Britain is England, Scotland and Wales. The United Kingdom adds Northern Ireland.
